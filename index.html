A working log from HeyMax

The self-driving ops team

What happens when a five-person operations team learns to run itself.

Vinit Nair·Head of Operations, HeyMax·Singapore·Updated Aug 2026

A five-person operations team at HeyMax — SEA's fastest-growing loyalty fintech — runs a $5M+ GMV commerce business across voucher inventory, affiliate deals, and merchant escalations. Over the past year we removed 300+ hours of manual work a month. Campaign volume more than doubled. Defects per campaign fell.

Underneath it: agents that draft campaign deliverables, validate each other's work against versioned specs, and stop the line when something looks wrong, with a human accountable at every launch.

I'm Vinit. I run operations at HeyMax and built this system myself, with Claude, a custom MCP server, and n8n.

AI agents for campaign operations

Every merchant campaign — voucher drops, tiered upsizes, partner promos — needed manual coordination across briefs, assets, validation, and launch. Growth meant hiring more coordinators or building a system that runs the process itself.

We built Fulfilment Autopilot: campaign briefs arrive through structured intake, a builder agent drafts every deliverable from versioned skill documents, an independent validator agent checks the output, and a compulsory Go/No-Go gate keeps a human accountable for every launch. It runs on a 104-function MCP server, orchestrated with n8n, with Linear as the system of record. Roughly 30% of campaign projects now run in the auto lane, and that share is roughly doubling month over month.

We started piloting agents for campaign setup at the end of March. The curve didn't jump — it compounded: campaign launches held around 50–60 a month through the first half of the year, then hit 76 in June and 122 in July, even as agents started taking on more complex campaigns from mid-June. Same five people.

We audit every launch afterwards against launch records. Each thing the gates stop is logged, resolved, and folded back into the skill specs the agents build from, so the same mistake doesn't need catching twice.

Before Autopilot, campaigns escaped roughly 0.6–0.7 defects each. With the gates live that rate fell and stayed down, trending lower for ten straight campaigns before a single launch put three escapes into production and pulled it back to 0.40. The regression is in the data on purpose: the audit exists to catch it, and the postmortem feeds the validator's spec.

Don't ask if the agent can decide. Ask who decides today.

Most AI workflow design stalls at the same spot: a step requires judgment, so a human gate goes in and gets called safety. I ask a different question first: who is making this decision today?

Map every judgment call on two axes: how expensive is being wrong, and who handles it today — a critical thinker, or a routine worker following a pattern?

Three of the four quadrants are handoff candidates: the agent works from the same information the human had and can be assumed to decide at least as well. That holds in the fourth quadrant too — it's just less palatable, so it goes last. Which is why Autopilot's only mandatory human gate is the Go/No-Go. Everything below it was already being decided on pattern.

Designing processes for better customer experience

Rewards that don't show up are the loyalty industry's most notorious trust killer. A customer shops, the affiliate network fails to track the transaction, and the user spends weeks chasing something nobody can see. It was a top driver of support volume and dissatisfaction.

We often can't resolve these quickly. Verification depends on the merchant and the network, takes weeks, and sometimes ends in a denial. What we could change was the wait. A customer who reports missing miles now sees pending Max Miles in their balance immediately. The miles aren't guaranteed — they can still be reversed if the transaction never validates — but the claim is visibly acknowledged instead of invisible.

The underlying process never got faster. Only the experience of waiting changed. Support workflows behind it (Intercom + n8n + OpenAI) handle triage and status so the queue doesn't reintroduce the silence.

Daily checklist and an ops on-call rotation to improve incident rates and response times

By early 2025 the business had more surfaces than one person could hold: voucher inventory across partners, live campaigns, support escalations, redemption SLAs, wallet balances. We had alerting on all of it. Alerts were not the problem.

An alert competes with whatever you're already doing, and it usually loses. A wallet warning at 11am gets acknowledged, filed, and deferred behind something louder, and nothing distinguishes "seen and handled" from "seen and forgotten." Escalation was working as designed and still failing.

So in January 2025 we introduced a daily ops checklist: every key fulfilment surface checked and ticked off, every day, by a named owner. Not new detection — a forcing function on what we already had. An unchecked item is now visible as an unchecked item, not invisible as an unread notification.

The checklist handles what we can anticipate. What breaks at 9pm goes to a named ops on-call rotation, so one person is accountable for response instead of a channel where everyone assumes someone else has it.

Both have run every day since. Incidents fell by roughly 40%, and response times stopped depending on who happened to be looking.

It's the least sophisticated thing on this page and one of the highest-return. The failure wasn't a missing capability, so building more capability wouldn't have fixed it. Automating first would have added a system on top of a system and left the actual gap — attention, and accountability for it — untouched.

How this is built

Build

Claude (agents, skills, MCP), n8n orchestration, Intercom + OpenAI support automation, Appsmith internal tools.

Measure

BigQuery/SQL. Every launch audited against launch records — defects confirmed, attributed, and published, escapes included.

Operate

Playbooks, validation gates, OKR baselines pulled from live systems. Five people, $5M+ GMV across vouchers, affiliate deals, and merchant escalations.
